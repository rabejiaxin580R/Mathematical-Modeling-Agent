Summary Sheet

The International Olympic Committee (IOC) is organizing the 2032 Summer Olympics in Brisbane,
Australia, with the aim of keeping the Games relevant and appealing to a global audience. To help determine
which Sports, Disciplines, and Events (SDEs) should be added or removed, our team  developed a
comprehensive model to assess SDEs against six IOC criteria: Popularity and Accessibility, Gender Equity,
Sustainability, Inclusivity, Relevance and Innovation, and Safety and Fair Play.
We identified 27 quantitative and qualitative sub-criteria/factors, such as ticket sales, digital media
engagement, leadership representation, environmental policies, global reach, youth appeal, and safety
measures like injury rates and anti-doping compliance. Additionally, some of these factors are specific to the
host country, such as cultur al relevance. These factors provided a robust and comprehensive  basis for
evaluating each SDE's alignment with the IOC’s vision.
With these 27 factors, we developed a Hierarchical Multi-Criteria Decision Model (H-MCDM). Our
H-MCDM provides a structured, transparent, and adaptable framework for quantitatively assessing each sport
against the IOC's criteria and accounting for global trends and host -specific factors. Our approach integrates
Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) and Technique for Order Preference by Similarity to Ideal Solution
(TOPSIS) within a hierarchical structure. AHP is used at the top level to determine weights to the six main
criteria based on their importance. At the sub-criteria level, predefined weights capture the specific relevance
of each factor. TOPSIS is then applied sequentially, first to assess SDE performance at the sub-criteria level,
then to aggregate scores at the main criteria and it then gives out an overall score to comprehensively measure
an SDE’s alignment with IOC criteria . The higher the score, the better the alignment with IOC criteria.  A
comprehensive ranking of SDEs can then be generated.
We tested our model on eleven SDEs with a known status for the three Olympics: Tokyo 2020, Paris
2024, and LA 2028. We chose a diverse range of SDEs, including eight sports that have been recently added
or removed (Skateboarding, Sport Climbing, Breaking, Karate, Flag Football, Lacrosse Sixes, Coastal
Rowing, and Baseball/Softball), as well as three that have been in the Olympic program since the 1988 Games
or earlier (Athletics, Swimming, and Gymnastics). Athletics, Gymnastics, and Swimming consistently ranked
highest, reaffirming their global appeal and historical significance. The model effectively adapts to varying
cultural, regional, and strategic priorities of dif ferent host cities. For example, our model assigned karate a
relatively high score of 0.5607 and a high rank of #4 for Tokyo 2020. However, for Paris 2024 and LA 2028,
the model outputted scores of 0.3369 and 0.2621 with ranks of #10 and #11, respectively. The alignment of
high-ranked sports with actual Olympic decisions validates our model's criteria and weighting approach.
We then applied our model to evaluate the suitability of the eight recently added or removed SDEs for
the 2032 Brisbane Olympics. Apart from the core sports, the  top three ranked SDEs  are Skateboarding,
Breaking and Sport Climbing. Therefore, we recommend that these three SDEs be considered for inclusion
in the Brisbane 2032 Olympic Summer Games. For future inclusion in the year 2036 and beyond, we suggest
Padel, Pickleball and VR-archery based on international growth and increasing popularity.
We conducted sensitivity analyses by adjusting the weights of the main criteria by ±10% and ±50%. The
model remained stable, with core sports maintaining top rankings and recommended sports consistently
scoring highly. It also shows that Popularity and Accessibility and Relevance and Innovation are the most
influential criteria, ensuring the model reflects IOC strategic priorities. Lower -weighted criteria like
Sustainability and Safety and Fair Play had a balanced impact, confirming the model's reliability across
various scenarios. In conclusion, our H-MCDM offers a reliable, data-driven approach for evaluating SDEs
and supports informed decision-making that aligns with the IOC's evolving vision.

1 Introduction
1.1 Problem Background
The Olympic Games is the largest multi-sport event in the world, and the International Olympic Committee (IOC)
faces the continuous challenge of ensuring that they stay relevant, inclusive, and aligned with modern values. Over
time, this has involved adding, removing, or reintroducing Sports, Disciplines, and Events (SDEs) to reflect changing
cultural and global trends.
To guide these decisions, the IOC has established six criteria for Sports Inclusion: Popularity and Accessibility,
Gender Equity, Sustainability, Inclusivity, Relevance and Innovation, and Safety and Fair Play. However, applying
these criteria consistently and quantitatively across a wide range of sports is a complex task. Each SDE has unique
cultural, logistical, and historical factors that need to be considered, making it difficult to evaluate them.
In this paper, we aim to address this challenge by developing a mathematical model capable of quantitatively
assessing SDEs based on their alignment with the IOC’s criteria for Sports Inclusion.

2 Assumptions and Justifications
To simplify the problem of quantitatively evaluating SDEs in Olympic games, we make the following assumptions.
Assumption 1: Venue Capacity as a Proxy for Ticket Sales and Attendance
In the absence of specific attendance figures, we assume that ticket sales are approximately equal to the venue's
capacity.
Justification: Major sporting events, particularly high-profile games or finals, tend to attract audiences that fill the
venue to its maximum capacity.
Assumption 2: Using 2023 World Championship Venue Capacity for Ticket Sales and Attendance Figures
We assume that the 2023 World Championship attendance data is the most recent and relevant.
Justification: Many sports hold their World Championships every two years, meaning 2023 is the latest available
data until 2025. The 2023 championships provide the most current attendance information, reflecting recent audience
interest and ticket sales.
Assumption 3: Estimating Attendance for last Coastal Rowing World Championship
For Coastal Rowing, where there is no ticketed venue, we assume an estimated attendance of 5,000 spectators.
Justification: Coastal rowing events are open to public viewing along shorelines, often attracting thousands of in-
formal spectators. Based on similar outdoor events, an estimated value of 5,000 is a reasonable approximation for
attendance.
Assumption 4: Using YouTube View Data as a Proxy for Digital Media Engagement
We assume that YouTube metrics (e.g., views) for each sport's 2023 World Championship can be used as a reliable
indicator of overall digital media engagement.
Justification: YouTube is a major platform for sports content consumption, especially for large -scale international
events. Metrics like views provide valuable insights into audience interest and popularity.
Assumption 5: Equal Events and Participation
We assume that all sports considered in this analysis will either maintain or transition toward gender equality in
events and participation, in line with the IOC’s commitment to gender equality.
Justification: Current Olympic sports have increasingly demonstrated gender balance in recent Games. For sports
not yet included in the Olympics, we assume that compliance with IOC policies will ensure gender parity in partici-
pation and events.

criteria and sub-criteria, ensuring a comprehensive analysis aligned w ith the IOC's vision  (Figure 3). We will first

7 Sensitivity Analysis

cial for supporting accurate decisions and determining the reliability of results. We test the sensitivity of our model
by adjusting the weights of the top-level criteria and analyzing their influence on the rankings of the alternatives.
Specifically, we altered the weight of one criterion at a time up to 10% or 50%, while holding the others constant
to identify the factors that most significantly influence the rankings. Due to the page limitation, we only presented
our sensitivity analysis results using Tokyo as the host, but the results are consistent for other host cities.

Sustainability, and Safety and Fair Play, have minimal impact on the rankings. The model demonstrates stability,
with Athletics, Gymnastics, and Swimming consistently dominating the top ranks. Youth-focused sports like
Skateboarding, Sport Climbing, and Breaking show moderate changes in scores when criteria like Relevance and
Innovation are adjusted, reflecting a clear connection to those factors rather than random shifts. Overall, the model
is balanced and resilient to weight variations, showing it is robust.

7.3 Model Strengths and Weaknesses
The Hierarchical Multi-Criteria Decision Model effectively integrates AHP and TOPSIS within a hierarchical
framework to evaluate and rank SDEs for Olympic inclusion. By systematically considering both the relative im-
portance of criteria (through AHP) and the performance of each SDE against these criteria (through TOPSIS), the
model provides a robust and transparent tool for decision-makers. This approach ensures that the selected SDEs
align closely with the IOC's evolving vision and strategic objectives. More specifically, we listed our model’s
strengths and weaknesses below.
7.3.1 Strengths
• Structured Evaluation: Reflects the multi-level nature of the IOC's criteria, ensuring that both high-level
and detailed factors are considered.
• Comprehensive Evaluation: By combining multiple criteria, the model ensures a balanced and holistic
assessment of sports.
• Flexibility: Can accommodate changes in criteria or the addition of new sub-criteria without significantly
impacting the entire model.
• Transparency: Each step of the evaluation is clear and can be examined individually, aiding in under-
standing and acceptance of the results.
• Stability: Sensitivity analysis shows that the model remains stable with changes in criteria weights, ensur-
ing reliability in rankings.
7.3.2 Weaknesses
• Dependence on Data Quality: The accuracy of rankings heavily depends on the availability and reliability
of input data.
• Underrepresentation of Low-Weighted Criteria: Lower-weighted criteria like Sustainability and Safety
and Fair Play may have limited influence on rankings as shown in our sensitivity analysis.
• Subjectivity in Weight Assignment: While AHP helps determine weights, the initial pairwise compari-
sons rely on subjective judgments, which may introduce bias. Also, pre-defined weights for sub-criteria
introduce subjective biases that may affect the outcomes.
