Summary Sheet

Every four years, each Olympic Games sees the introduction, removal, or reintroduction of certain sports,
disciplines or events (SDEs). Our team aims to determine whether a SDE wi ll be included in the Olympics
and developed a model scoring each SDE, while the score shows the potential of SDEs being selected.
In Task 1, we conducted factor analysis based on the six IOC criteria and identified 21 factors, along with
their units and characteristics.
In Task 2, we used the 21 factors to construct the evaluation model. Given our data ha s relatively high
dimensions while no assumptions on it, we adopted Technique for Order Preference by Similarity to Ideal
Solution (TOPSIS) as our basic model, which aligns best  with our data characteristics. To optimize model
performance, we incorporated weight calculations into traditional TOPSIS method. First, we used the entropy
weight method (EWM) to calculate the weights of each factor, and thereby obtain a score fro m TOPSIS
integrating the weight. Next, we repeated the process using the Analytical Hierarchy Process (AHP) method
to derive a second score. Finally, a weighted average of the two scores produces the final score , 0.6 for the
first and 0.4 for the second. After the model is constructed, we confirmed its feasibility by inputting simulated
data into it. Also, we set a threshold of inclusion as 0.45, based on the distribution of the simulation results
For Task 3, we selected three recently added SDEs (Sport Climbing, Skateboarding, and Surfing) and three
long-standing SDEs (Swimming, Artistic Gymnastics, and Fencing) in the recent four Olympic Games as test
subjects. After collecting data and inputting it into our model, we found new SDEs all scored above the given
threshold 0.45, all scoring high on Popularity and Accessibility principle, especially Skateboarding; while
the old SDEs were also steady above the threshold with a gradual increasing trend, scoring similarly well on
all 6 principles. These results align with real-world trends, demonstrating the generality of our model.
In Task 4, we evaluated SDEs for future Olympics. Field Handball, Teqball, Rugby Union, and L eague of
Legends were for 2032. B y inputting estimated data into the model, we concluded that scores for Field
Handball, Teqball, and League of Legends all indicated potential inclusion in 2032. The ranking was Teqball >
League of Legends > Field Handball, with Teqball scoring significantly high on Relevance and Innovation.
Next, we identified Baseball and Squash as potential candidates for the 2036 Olympics, performing similar
process to compute their scores. The model yielded scores above the threshold fo r both, suggesting they
both have the potential of being selected.
In Task 5, we conducted sensitivity analyses on both single and multiple factors. First, we performed single-
variable analysis on the 21 factors and plotted the results, concluding that Continental Spread , Injury
Probability, and F/M Athlete Ratio were the most sensitive factors. Then, we examined intersection effects
between variables, selected two pairs of factors for multifactor analysis and plotted their results. Next, we
conducted analysis for each of the six principles, finding that Inclusivity was the most sensitive among
them. Lastly, since we used a weighted average method to calculate scores, we also performed a sensitivity
analysis on the two weights used in the model.
Finally, we wrote a letter to the IOC presenting our model and findings.
Keywords:  TOPSIS method, Entropy Weight method, Analytical Hierarchy Process method,
Weighted Average method, Threshold of Inclusion, Single and Multiple Factor Sensitivity Analysis

1.Introduction
1.1. Background
The Olympic Games have long served as the world's most powerful symbol of athletic
values and diversity. But as the dynamics of global sports and audience interest is
constantly changing, the International Olympic Committee (IOC) had been
continuously revising its evaluation process used to decide suitable sports, disciplines
and events (SDEs), so that tournaments can match with the role. In the 2024 Paris
Games, breaking made its debut, but the LA28 Games Plan also showed it would not
be included in the upcoming Olympics. This obviously reflects how evaluation criteria
changes rapidly, thus a sensible evaluation method that is able to accommodate the
changes is crucial to a successful Olympics.
1.2. Problem Restatement
a) Identify features of SDEs, along with the six IOC criteria,  give choice reasons
and include characteristics and units.
b) Develop a model to evaluate which of the various SDEs align best with the current
Olympic criteria.
c) Test model on 3 recently appeared SDEs and 3 long-existed SDEs. Then compare
the model's outcome with the actual result and evaluate if the model aligns with it.
d) Predict 3 suitable SDEs that can be added or reintroduced to the 2032 Brisbane
Olympics and rate them first, second and third. Then consider further beyond 2032.
e) Perform sensitivity analysis on the model and identify which factors are most
important to the decisions , then evaluate whether the significance of these factors
are advantageous or disadvantageous of the model.
f) Draft a letter addressed to the IOC reporting our conclusions.

2.3. Evaluation Model for SDEs (Task2)
2.3.1. General Structure
In order to evaluate whether a SDE  can be included in the Olympics , and considering
that we have collected data on 21 factors, which is quite a l ot, we conclude a multi-

dimensional evaluation problem. Therefore, we adopted the Technique for Order
Preference by Similarity to Ideal Solution (TOPSIS) method as our basic model for the
following reasons:
1. Multi-Dimensional Evaluation
TOPSIS considers multi-dimensional data by defining the positive ideal solution (the
optimal scenario) and the negative ideal solution (the worst scenario), and evaluate
SDEs based on its distance from these two ideal solutions.
2. High Interpretability
TOPSIS provides good interpretability, which is particularly necessary for the
Olympics to everyone interested in the games why certain projects were included or
excluded.
3. Low Data Requirements
TOPSIS does not require any assumptions or statistical analysis of the data, and only
requires the weights of each attribute and the data of various factors.

However, we found that relying solely on EWM has some limitations:
1. Some factors rely on subjective ratings of  assigned scores. As a result, the
degree of dispersion for these factors  are all small, lea ding to small weights for
them that does not fit reality.
2. Some variables, such as whether participatin g countries exceed 75, are
prerequisites so all SDEs have the same value of them. And the entropy weights
of these factors become  0. Although we introduced a small perturbation in
computation to prevent their weights from being exactly 0, but these weights are
still very small.
So we concluded that another different model is needed to calculate the weights. And
AHP (Analytic Hierarchy Process), a method based on subjective ratings to calculate
weights, the opposite of entropy weight method, was the solution we chose.

3. Strengths and Weaknesses
3.1. Strengths
1. Our model considers 21 variables, making it relatively comprehensive.
2. Our final model integrates three widely used evaluation models. Each of these
models has the  capability to independently produce a scoring result , while w e
ᇿ Hi MCM
ሧ࿟
ğ
selected them based on their  respective strengths and ultimately combined them
into a single model, demonstrating a degree of innovation.
3. We utilized the entropy weight method to mitigate the subjectivity inherent in the
AHP method while simultaneously using the AHP method to offset the data
dependency of the entropy weight method. This approach ensures that the results
are relatively objective and accurate.
3.2. Weaknesses
1. We assumed that SDEs do not influence each other, which allowed us to ignore
complex and difficult-to-quantify factors (such as the similarity and redundancy of
events). However, this also means that the factors considered in our model are not
comprehensive enough.
2. The two models we used to calculate weights each have their own limitations:
3. The entropy weight method heavily relies on the collected data to calculate factor
weights.
1) However, since we do not have access to authoritative databases and had
to search online, our sample data is still somewhat incomplete and
inaccurate, which could likely reduce the accuracy of the model.
2) When using the AHP model for weight calculation, the weights are
entirely assigned subjectively, which makes the method highly subjective
and could negatively impact the objectivity of our model.
4. Even though we combined the two methods to mitigate these limitations to some
extent, it is still impossible to eliminate these shortcomings entirely. Therefore, this
remains a significant drawback.
5. The method we used to combine TE_Score and TA_Score is relatively simple. As
mentioned in Section 2.6.4, the weight that maximizes the final score can vary for
each SDE based on its respective scores. But a ssigning different weights to each
SDE for calculating the final score is clearly impractical and we still chose to assign
a fixed weight of 60% for TE_Score  and 40% for TA_Score  across all SDEs.
However, this approach prevents us from maximizing the score for each SDE,
introducing another limitation.
